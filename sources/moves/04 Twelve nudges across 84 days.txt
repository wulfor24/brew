WHY THIS MATTERS FOR ARATA
This discipline was written for software. For Arata it means the beliefs a person holds before, during
and after buying a ₹571 serum, and the small design choices on the ad, the product page, the cart, the
box and the WhatsApp message that move those beliefs. Nothing here needs a new product. It needs
the existing product presented in the order a worried human actually thinks.
Arata's customer arrives already burned. The brief is clear on this: by the time Hair-fall Rahul lands on
arata.in he has spent ₹2,000 to ₹5,000 on three to five products that did nothing, including onion
shampoo and biotin gummies. He is afraid of minoxidil. He finds Traya expensive and exhausting. His
operating belief is "claims are lies" and his operating fear is "it is too late". That is the mind every Arata
asset is talking to.
Arata's answer to that mind is a number on every bottle: 7.4X, 28,200, 95%, 86%, 87%. This is the
right instinct. Specific numbers from studies are the strongest trust signal in the ₹400 to ₹700 band,
and nobody else there does it. But a number read by a sceptic can backfire. "Up to 28,200 new hairs"
sits in the same slot in his head as "100% regrowth guaranteed" unless the page does the work of
making it believable. The brief already lists "claims sound too good" as an objection. Today the page
states the number and moves on.
The second problem is what happens after the first order. Every hero claim is measured at 84 days. A
single 30 ml bottle lasts about a month (assumption). So the product only proves itself to customers
who buy three times, and nothing in the current journey makes that happen: no subscription, no
routine builder, no reorder prompt, no expectation set for the slow weeks. The industry repeat rate for
beauty is about 26%. Routine-led models pass 40%. The 14-point gap is almost entirely psychology:
defaults, commitment, progress and timing.
The third problem is the box. Reviews say "the seal was broken", "the shampoo bottle was leaking".
People judge an experience by its peak and its end. For a D2C order the end is the box opening. A leak
at that moment erases the number on the bottle. The fix is partly operations and partly a promise the

MitoActive page that earns its first reviews instead of waiting for them.

SECOND-ORDER TRAPS ALREADY IN THE SYSTEM
Three things that work today and will cost Arata later.
Permanent discounts. The Pack of 3 is always 49% off. The customer learns that ₹1,947 is fiction and
₹999 is the price. Once learned, any future "offer" has nothing to push against. The fix is to stop
calling ₹999 a discount and start calling it the plan price for 84 days.
Celebrity recall without product recall. The Samay Raina podcast built brand recall, which the founders
say was the goal. The second-order risk is that the recalled brand is "the funny one", and a funny
brand asks the sceptic to re-verify the science. The science assets (proof block, Lab Notes, Y100
studies) must be one click from every culture asset.
"Up to" claims. "Up to 95% reduction" is legally careful and psychologically expensive. A sceptic
reads "up to" as "probably not me". Left alone, it lowers belief. Explained, it raises it, because an
honest limit is the strongest signal that the rest is true.

INVERSION: WHAT WOULD GUARANTEE FAILURE
If we wanted Arata's hair growth line to fail for a sceptical 28-year-old, we would: show the biggest
number first with no study beside it; give him five ways to buy the same outcome; show the price with
no comparison; say nothing about the first three weeks; ship a box with no card; stay silent for 84
days; let the bottle run out without a message; and make the discount permanent so the next one
means nothing. Arata does six of these eight today. The plan removes all eight.



THE PLAN
THREE PRINCIPLES
One unit of time. 84 days is already on the pack. Every nudge, price frame, message and progress bar
uses it. The customer should never think in bottles. They think in days.
One default. Every concern collection gets one pre-selected routine. Choice stays available. The
decision is removed.
Proof before promise. On every surface the order is: what the customer already knows → the study →
the reviews → the number. Never the number first.

Per-day arithmetic (assumption: one 30 ml bottle lasts 28 days at the stated usage): ₹571 ÷ 28 = ₹20 a
day. ₹999 ÷ 84 = ₹12 a day. ₹1,499 ÷ 30 = ₹50 a day.
The single bottle is the decoy. At ₹20 a day for a claim that needs 84 days, it exists to make the ₹12-a-
day routine the obvious choice. MitoActive sits above as the premium anchor, which also makes ₹999
feel modest. For MitoActive itself, the anchor is external: Traya's monthly kit. Put that comparison on
the page.
On the ₹1,499 sticker: a 9-ending pulls a product toward "value" in the reader's mind, and MitoActive's
story is longevity science. We do not recommend changing the sticker, because marketplaces price-
compare on it. The per-day frame does the premium work instead.
On discounts: in rupees, the absolute saving is almost always the bigger-looking number. "Save ₹948"
replaces "49% off" on the Pack of 3. "Save ₹480" replaces "20% off" where it applies. The exception
is combos that save under ₹100, where neither number impresses and the discount should become a
named bonus instead.

WHERE TO BE BOLD AND WHERE TO BE SAFE
Apply a barbell. Keep the claims side extremely safe: every number with its study, its panel and its
limit, nothing a derm could quarrel with. Spend the risk budget on culture: the Samay Raina style of
media, the mischievous tone, the mock podcast. The two must link. A culture asset with no science
one click away wastes the recall it built. A science page with no personality reads like Minimalist.

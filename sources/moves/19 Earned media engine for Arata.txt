WHY THIS MATTERS FOR ARATA
The software version of this discipline chases TechCrunch. The Arata version chases Economic Times
Brand Equity, Mint, Inc42, Storyboard18, Vogue India, HT Lifestyle, a trichologist on a Mumbai
podcast, and the answer ChatGPT gives when someone types "best hair fall serum in India". Same
method, different rooms.
Arata's press record today is milestone-shaped. BeautyMatter covered the $4M Series A. BW Retail
World covered the MitoActive launch on 12 August 2026. Storyboard18 covered the Samay Raina mock
podcast. StopDown wrote up results in February. Every one of those stories happened because
something happened to the company. None of them happened because Arata had something to say.
That is the gap: the brand has a founder who talks about "real estate in people's minds", a lab in Delhi,
a number on every bottle, and 1.5 million customers a year, and it has never turned any of that into a
story that runs when there is no funding round.
The cost shows up in three places. First, MitoActive. It is a ₹1,499 serum with a mechanism nobody
else in India sells, backed by 5+ clinical studies, and two months after launch it has zero reviews and
one trade write-up. Second, the rebrand. Arata is moving from "zero chemicals" to "Hair & Scalp
Science Co." The old identity is still on marketplace listings (Super Shampoo with onion, Curl Care)
and in old press. Without a new press record, a journalist searching the brand in 2027 finds the plant-
based story from 2019. Third, the claims. 7.4X, 28,200 new hairs, 95%, 86%, 87%. These are Arata's
biggest differentiator and its biggest exposure. Any one of them could be challenged publicly, and
today there is no public dossier, no spokesperson plan and no holding statement.
The opportunity is that Arata can out-earn what it cannot outspend. Traya spends ₹138 Cr on
marketing. Arata's whole FY25 revenue was ₹61 Cr. The ₹160 Cr target needs brand search to grow
faster than paid reach can buy it, and earned media is the cheapest input to brand search, to
marketplace conversion, and to the AI answers that now sit between a customer and the "hair fall
serum" query. 68% of revenue lives on rented land. Press is one of the few assets Arata owns
outright.

Why: Arata is the only brand in the ₹400 to ₹700 band with 1.5M customers a year and a lab. Nobody
else can publish this. A journalist builds a whole piece around one surprising number, and the stat gets
re-cited in AI answers for years. It makes Arata the source of record for Indian hair fall.

ENGINE 2: THE ENEMY IS GUESSWORK (FOUNDER OP-EDS)
What: a founder op-ed every quarter, each against a broken norm, never a competitor. Quarter 1:
"India spends ₹5,000 to learn a shampoo does not work." Quarter 2: "Ingredient percentages are a
recipe, not a result." Quarter 3: "The minoxidil-or-nothing trap." Quarter 4: "What a hair claim should
have to show before it goes on a bottle." Each one ends with Arata's own dossier open for inspection.
Why: a principled fight is a press cycle and a policy conversation. It also protects the claims: a brand
that publicly asks for stricter claim standards is hard to accuse of inflating its own. The founders are
already on record wanting recall and honesty; this gives that voice a destination.
How: Brew drafts with the founder, places in marketing trade first (afaqs!, Storyboard18 and BW
Marketing World run contributed pieces), then pitches the sharper version to ET or Mint. Every op-ed
goes on Lab Notes the same week, which moves Lab Notes from seven posts in one March burst to a
cadence.
Expected effect: one placement a quarter, a quotable founder position every newsjack can reference,
and a public trail.

ENGINE 3: THE SCIENCE (MITOACTIVE, Y100, DERM VOICES)
What: a plain-language explainer on mitochondria and the anagen/telogen ratio written for a beauty
editor who has never read a study; a one-page Y100 summary (6+ years of research, 5+ clinical
studies, an Indian-participant study showing reduced shedding, improved anagen/telogen ratio,
increased density); a retained dermatologist or trichologist available for quotes within 24 hours.
Why: MitoActive has zero reviews and one trade mention. Beauty editors cover "longevity science

How: an exclusive to one beauty title, timed with the first 84-day customer results (early November
2026 for August buyers). Then a derm-podcast circuit with the R&D lead.
Expected effect: the first consumer-press coverage of MitoActive, a citable explainer that AI assistants
pick up, and a review base that starts from editorial rather than ads.

EXAMPLE 3: FOUNDER OP-ED, 650 WORDS, FOR MARKETING TRADE FIRST, THEN ET OR MINT
India spends ₹5,000 to find out a shampoo does not work
By Dhruv Madhok, co-founder, Arata
Here is the average journey of someone in India who notices more hair on the pillow. They try the
family shampoo for a month. Then onion. Then biotin gummies. Then a serum with a Korean story and
three ingredients in bold on the front. Somewhere around product four they start to believe it is too
late.
By our estimate that journey costs ₹2,000 to ₹5,000 and six to twelve months. 82 million urban Indians
have hair loss. 59 million of them are using home remedies. About a million have seen a clinician. The
rest are in the middle, buying on hope.
The problem is how the category makes claims. Walk through any marketplace listing in the ₹400 to
₹700 band and you will see the same sentence: "3% Redensyl, 4% Anagain, 1% Procapil."
Percentages. They look like proof. They are a recipe. A percentage tells you what is in the bottle. It
tells you nothing about what happened to anyone's hair.
The other end of the market has the opposite problem. Diagnosis platforms and prescription routines
show real evidence and then charge ₹1,400 to ₹1,800 a month for five to nine months, with doctor
calls, supplements and minoxidil. That is a medical path, and for many people it is the right one. For
most of the 82 million it is too much commitment for a problem that started three weeks ago.
What should sit in the middle is simple: a product that was tested on hair, with the result printed where
you can see it, with the window in which to expect it, and with the study design one click away.
That last part is the one the industry avoids. I will say what our own numbers mean, because I think
every brand should have to.
Our anti-hair fall shampoo says 7.4X hair fall reduction. That came from a tensile strength study, which
measures how much force it takes to break a strand treated with the active versus an untreated one. It
is a lab number, and we say so. Our growth serum says "up to 28,200 new hair in 84 days." That is a


BREW DEPLOYMENT TEAM × ARATA · EARNED MEDIA ENGINE FOR ARATA                                            9
consumer study, "up to" means the best responders, and 84 days is the full window of twice-daily
use. If you use it for two weeks and see nothing, the claim did not fail. The timeline did.
Nobody should take those numbers on faith. The category should publish its study designs next to its
claims, so a 28-year-old in Bangalore can compare them the way he compares phone specs.
Three things would fix most of this. First, every outcome claim should carry its study type on the pack:
in-vitro, consumer, clinical. Those are different kinds of evidence and the customer deserves to know
which one she is looking at. Second, "up to" should always come with a window. A number without a
timeline is a lottery ticket. Third, the regulator and the industry body should treat a percentage on the
front of pack as a formulation disclosure, not a result, and say so in the guidance.
We built a lab in Delhi because Indian hair in Indian water in Indian humidity behaves differently from
the hair the global studies were run on, and because we wanted our own numbers rather than a
supplier's brochure. That is a cost. It is also the only way I know to put a number on a bottle and sleep.
The customer has already paid ₹5,000 for guesswork. The least the category can do is show its
working.
Arata's full claims dossier, with study design for every number on every pack, is at arata.in/press.

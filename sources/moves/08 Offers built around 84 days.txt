WHY THIS MATTERS FOR ARATA
Offer design was written for services and courses. For Arata it means the thing a customer buys in one
click: the bundle, the gift, the promise if it fails, the reason to buy today, the name they tell a friend,
and how they pay. Everything else (ad, page, email) only carries that offer.
Arata has the hardest part done. Every hero bottle carries a number from a study: 7.4X, 28,200, 95%,
86%, 87%. Nobody else in the ₹400 to ₹700 band does that. But the number sits on a bottle, and a
bottle is a weak offer. The customer still has to decide which serum, whether to add the shampoo,
whether 30 ml is enough, whether to trust a brand that zero people have reviewed on its ₹1,499 hero,
and what happens if it leaks. Each of those is a decision the customer makes alone, and every
decision is a place to leave the cart.
Look at what arata.in sells today. Single SKUs at ₹199 to ₹1,499. Combos at 7% to 49% off. Free
shipping above ₹299. A free gift above ₹999 that is never named. No subscription. No guarantee
anyone can find. No reason to buy this week instead of next. The only lever being pulled is the
discount, and the biggest discount (49% off the Hair Growth Intensive Serum Pack of 3) is the closest
thing Arata has to a real offer. It is the right product, three bottles, exactly the 84 days the claim needs.
It is called "Pack of 3".
That costs Arata in three places. First, conversion: ~650K monthly visits and 50K D2C orders means a
visit-to-order rate near 7% to 8% including returners, which is healthy, but the brand is spending that
traffic on single bottles when the claim only works on three. Second, repeat: the business goal is a
repeat purchase within 45 days, and nothing in the offer makes the second bottle automatic. Beauty
repeat rates sit near 26%; routine-led models go past 40%. Third, MitoActive: a ₹1,499 serum with
zero reviews two months after launch has a perceived-likelihood problem that no amount of Y100
explainer content solves. Only finished 84-day journeys solve it.
The opportunity is to stop selling bottles and start selling plans. The 84-day window is already on the
pack. Build the offer around it: a named plan, a guarantee that matches the claim, bonuses that close
the exact objections in the reviews, honest scarcity for the launch, and a way to pay monthly. The
price barely moves. The structure does.

Decision rules
    Plan share above 25% at day 90 and invocation under 5%: extend the plan frame to Anti-Grey (a 3-
    month claim, "86% reduction in greys", fits an 84-Day Grey Plan) and to the Damage Defence
    range for wedding-season gifting.
    Plan share under 15%: the per-day framing and the guarantee are not the problem; the default on
    the collection page is. Make the plan the only hair fall product on the first screen and move single
    bottles below.
    Invocation above 8% on the photo promise: do not remove the guarantee. Add the day 42 photo as
    a third checkpoint and route anyone showing no change at day 42 to the lab team early. If
    invocation stays above 8% after that, the claim window on the page moves from "hair fall reduced"
    to "hair fall reduced or new growth visible", and we revisit the study language with Arata's R&D.
    Monthly-billing take rate under 20%: the ₹549 × 3 structure is being read as a subscription trap.
    Rename it "ship monthly, stop anytime" and move the cancel line above the button.
    Founding 500 sells out before day 45: do not extend it. Close the bonuses on schedule, publish the
    first results set at day 84 of the first buyers, and open "Founding 500" as a closed chapter with its
    own page. That is what makes the next scarcity believable.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR ARATA
Arata has one decision sitting under every other marketing decision this year. The rebrand to "Hair &
Scalp Science Co." is underway. MitoActive soft-launched in August 2026 at ₹1,499 with zero reviews.
The stated goal is to make MitoActive the hero. And 50,000 D2C orders a month are still being won by
a ₹359 shampoo and a ₹571 serum that customers describe as "affordable and actually works". Those
two facts pull in opposite directions. Until the brand decides what its face is, every ad, every PDP,
every Blinkit listing and every homepage hero is a compromise between them.
The cost of leaving it open is real. The Hair Growth collection today mixes a 28,200-new-hairs claim, a
7.4X claim and an 87%-in-84-days claim with no routine builder and no ladder. A customer landing
from a Meta ad sees two growth serums, 2.6 times apart in price, one with 474 reviews and one with
none. If MitoActive becomes the default serum in ads, the brand is asking a new customer to pay
₹1,499 for a product nobody has publicly vouched for. If the Intensive Serum stays the face, the brand
keeps fighting Pilgrim at ₹595 to ₹699 and Minimalist at ₹699 on the same Redensyl shelf, and the
Y100 exclusivity goes unused.
This is the kind of question that benefits from being argued. So we ran it through a council. Five
lenses, chosen because they conflict: positioning, market sophistication, offer economics, brand
science, and behavioural signalling. Two were seated because their documented positions disagree
with where the brand is leaning. A council that agrees is a mirror.
The output is a decision, a ladder, a test plan and the assets to start. The short version: "face" and
"hero" are two different jobs, and Arata has been treating them as one.

SEAT 1: DUNFORD. POSITIONING BEFORE PRICE
The first question this lens asks: if Arata did not exist, what would the customer honestly do instead?
The answer splits by product, and that split is the whole problem.
The ₹571 Intensive Serum buyer's alternatives are Pilgrim at ₹595 to ₹699, Minimalist at ₹699, Bare
Anatomy at ₹649, and the status quo of onion shampoo and Sunday oiling. Same shelf, same actives
stack, same Nykaa search results. Arata's unique attribute on that shelf is the number (28,200) and
the Indian-hair framing. That is a head-to-head position in an existing category. Winnable, but by
inches.
The MitoActive buyer's alternatives are different: Traya at ₹1,400 to ₹1,800 a month for five months
with doctor calls and supplements, Mintop 5% minoxidil at ₹600 to ₹900 with dependency and
shedding, a dermatologist visit, or waiting and worrying. Arata's unique attribute against that set is a
new mechanism (mitochondrial function, anagen/telogen ratio) with Indian-participant data, no doctor,
no pills, and a ₹50-a-day price that sits level with Traya's monthly cost. That is a big-fish-small-pond
position: the clinical-grade result without the clinical-grade commitment.

The lens is firm on one risk. Naming a new category ("mitochondrial hair growth treatment") means
selling the problem and the solution. Most customers do not know they have a mitochondrial problem.
They know they have hair on the pillow. So the frame should be one the market already holds: "hair
growth treatment, without a doctor", with Y100 as the proof inside it. Category creation is the hardest
and rarest route and the brief's own customer language ("hair fall", "hair thinning") says the market
has not asked for a new word.
Second rule of this lens: start from the customers who love you. Those are the 474 Intensive Serum
reviewers and the 388 pack-of-3 reviewers. MitoActive has none yet, so its best-fit customer is a
hypothesis (Clinical Kavya). A brand cannot be repositioned around a hypothesis. It can be
repositioned around a proof point. MitoActive is the proof that Arata is a science company. The
Intensive Serum is where the loved customers live.
Bottom line: Position the brand, not a bottle. The position is "the hair science company that puts a
tested number on every product". MitoActive is the strongest evidence for that position. The Intensive
Serum is its most-bought expression. Decide the face on which product makes the position obvious,
and that is MitoActive.

SEAT 2: SCHWARTZ. SOPHISTICATION SAYS MECHANISM
This lens begins with the market's exposure to claims, not the product. The Indian hair growth serum
market is at the end of stage two and entering stage three. Stage two is "enlarge the claim": Pilgrim's
Redensyl 3% plus Anagain 4%, Minimalist's 18% actives, ThriveCo's "150% better growth in 8 weeks",
Arata's own 28,200. The percentages are getting bigger and the buyer is getting number-blind. The
brief's complaint column confirms it: "claims sound too good".
Stage three is where the claims are exhausted and the winner introduces a new mechanism. That is
exactly what Y100 is: a different "how it works" (mitochondria, not DHT blocking or follicle nutrients).
In a stage-three market, the mechanism becomes the headline because it makes the old claim
believable again. The 87% figure on its own is another percentage. "87%, because it works on the
follicle's energy supply, not on the surface" is a reason the buyer has not heard before.
On awareness, the lens is just as specific. Arata's traffic is mostly solution-aware and product-aware:
people who have tried onion shampoo, biotin gummies and probably one Redensyl serum. "Copy
cannot create desire for a product. It can only take the hopes, dreams, fears and desires that already
exist... and focus those already existing desires onto a particular product" (Breakthrough Advertising,
chapter one). The existing desire is new hair on the crown and a clean drain. Nobody wants
mitochondria. So the headline channels the desire in the market's own words, and the mechanism
arrives in the first sentence of body copy as the reason this time is different.
One more rule: never argue with the prospect's belief. The product-aware buyer believes "serums are
₹600 and they all do the same thing". Do not fight that. Agree with it. "You were right about those
serums. They used the same three actives. This one uses a different mechanism." That sentence puts
the Intensive Serum in the past tense for the sophisticated buyer and makes MitoActive the next thing.
Bottom line: Lead the advertising with MitoActive for the product-aware majority of hair-fall traffic,
because it is the only new mechanism on the shelf. Keep the headline in the customer's words (hair
fall, new hair, 84 days). This lens has nothing to say about whether ₹1,499 is right. That is the next
seat's job.

The numerator and denominator are nearly identical except one cell: perceived likelihood. MitoActive
has a stronger study and zero social proof. At ₹1,499 that is the whole conversion problem. The fix is
in the same cell: reviews, before-and-after at day 84, and a guarantee that moves the risk off the
buyer.
Next, the offer. This lens never competes on price and treats discounting as a signal of low value.
Which is the awkward truth about the current catalogue: the pack-of-3 Intensive Serum at ₹999 is
49% off and is the deepest discount Arata runs. That product already competes on price. It cannot be
the face of a premium science brand. MitoActive can, if it is sold as an offer rather than a bottle: an
84-day course (three bottles), the Anti-Hair Fall Shampoo thrown in, the ₹199 scalp massager thrown
in, a day-28 and day-56 check-in, and a plain promise: visible change by day 84 or your money back.
Stack the value until ₹3,999 (assumption, aligned with the pricing deliverable; list price of three bottles
is ₹4,497) feels obvious against Traya's ₹7,000 to ₹9,000 for five months.
Then the money model. Get cash: first order (shampoo + serum at ₹838, or MitoActive single). Get
more cash: upsell to the 84-day course on the thank-you page. Get the most cash: continuity. Arata
has no subscription. In this lens that is the missing third act, and MitoActive at ₹1,499 a month is the
product most worth a subscription.
Last, the market. The starving crowd is hair-fall panic: post-illness, post-partum, new city, hard water.
82 million urban Indians with hair loss. The crowd is there. The question is whether the offer is good
enough that saying no feels stupid.
Bottom line: Make the 84-day MitoActive course with a guarantee the headline offer, not the ₹1,499
single bottle. Spend the next 60 days on perceived likelihood (reviews, before-and-afters). Leave the
Intensive Serum as the cash-collecting front end.

SEAT 4: SHARP. THE DISSENTER ON REACH
This lens starts with the data the category keeps repeating. Brands grow by penetration: more buyers,
mostly light buyers, mostly people who buy the category rarely and buy several brands. Loyalty
follows share; it is not a lever a small brand can pull on its own. Arata at ₹61 Cr shares customers with
Mamaearth, Pilgrim, Dove and Head & Shoulders in proportion to their size. The buyers of a ₹1,499
serum are a thin slice of a thin slice.

So: who does a ₹1,499 face reach? The brief's own market map says 59 million urban Indians use
home remedies, 16 million use basic retail products, 5 million medicated OTC, 1 million clinical. A face
priced for the 1 to 5 million talks past the 75 million. And physical availability makes it worse. Thirty
percent of Arata's revenue comes from Zepto, Blinkit and Instamart, where the category entry point is
"hair is falling, what can I get in ten minutes". A ₹1,499 bottle is a considered purchase. A ₹359
shampoo is a basket add. If the brand's advertising teaches the market that Arata means ₹1,499, the
quick-commerce shelf gets harder, not easier.
Mental availability is built at category entry points, not around a flagship. Hair on the pillow after a
fever. Flakes in monsoon. A wedding in three weeks. A hard-water city. New mom. Which of those
does Arata come to mind for today? Nobody has measured it.
Then distinctiveness. The lens holds that buyers rarely perceive differentiation, and that mitochondria
versus Redensyl is exactly the kind of difference a light buyer never registers. What they register is
distinctive assets: the number on the bottle, the lab-blue pack, a name they can repeat. Naming is
inconsistent across channels today (onion vs biotin, serum vs tonic), the curl-care legacy range is still
live, and a full rebrand is underway. A rebrand that changes the pack while the hero changes too is
two memory resets in one year.
Bottom line: The face of the brand should be the brand, present at every entry point, consistently, at
the price most category buyers can act on. That is the ₹359 shampoo and ₹571 serum. MitoActive is a
fine SKU. Do not let it narrow who Arata talks to, and do not change the distinctive assets twice.

SEAT 5: SUTHERLAND. THE DISSENTER ON SIGNAL
This lens disagrees with the previous seat, and that is the point. The question it asks is psychological,
not logical: what does the price say?
₹571 says "another serum". It sits inside the ₹550 to ₹1,100 band where every Redensyl brand lives,
and a buyer who has been burned by two of them has no reason to believe a third. ₹1,499 says
something else. It is expensive enough to be a signal. Costly signalling holds that a message is
believed in proportion to what it costs to send. A brand that puts a ₹1,499 serum on its homepage is
saying "we are confident enough to charge this". That confidence transfers to the ₹359 shampoo
standing next to it. The opposite of a good idea can also be a good idea: make the expensive thing the
face so the affordable things feel like a bargain from a serious company, instead of making the
affordable thing the face and letting the expensive thing look like a mistake.
Anchoring works the same way. Put ₹1,499 at the top of the Hair Growth page and ₹571 reads as
reasonable and ₹999 for three reads as generous. Put ₹571 at the top and ₹1,499 reads as 2.6 times
too much. Same prices, different order, different behaviour.
The 30 ml problem is also perceptual. "₹1,499 for 30 ml" feels bad; "₹50 a day, less than the auto to
the derm" feels fine. And the 84-day wait is the single biggest psychological cost in this category. The
fix is a progress indicator, the way a map on a ride-hailing app reduces the pain of a wait without
shortening it: a weekly scalp photo, a shedding count, a day-28 message that says "this is the week
the fall slows".
One warning from this lens. Do not "efficiently" strip the things that make ₹1,499 feel like ₹1,499.
Reviews complain "it doesn't have the box". For a ₹359 shampoo the box is packaging. For a ₹1,499
serum the box is the product. Committees cut the box because it is cheap to cut and hard to measure.

And the strange word is an asset. "Mitochondria" is a little absurd on a shampoo shelf. Absurd gets
remembered. Rational converges on what Pilgrim already says.
Bottom line: MitoActive should be the face precisely because it is expensive and odd. Sell it as ₹50 a
day with a box, a ritual and a visible 84-day journey. Let its price make everything else look sensible.
